Percentages on the SAT

Problem-Solving & Data Analysis

Percent questions are the type most students get wrong — not because the math is hard, but because the wording hides a trap. Every percent question boils down to one question: is this percent describing the whole result, or just the amount of change? Get that right every time and every percent question on the SAT becomes the same handful of patterns, no matter how the numbers or the story around them change.

This lesson walks through those patterns in the order they show up on the test, from the basics up to the hardest "three-number chain" questions — using real SAT questions (some already in this practice set) worked all the way through, Desmos-style.

1. The Basics

Converting between percent and decimal, and what "of" means
To turn a decimal into a percent, ×100. To turn a percent into a decimal, ÷100.

So \(0.5 = 50\%\), and \(1.2345 = 123.45\%\). Notice that 1 is the same as 100% — that single fact is the key to almost everything later in this lesson.

The word "of" always means multiply.

"50% of 6" is just \(0.5 \times 6 = 3\). "75% of 2" is just \(0.75\times 2 = 1.5\). On the SAT's built-in Desmos calculator you can even type the percent symbol directly — type 50% and Desmos will offer "of" automatically and compute it for you. That's the entire idea behind a straight percent question: whenever a question just asks "what is X% of Y," you multiply, done.

The hard part is never this. The hard part is questions that don't just ask "X% of Y" — they describe an increase or a decrease. That's section 2, and it's the single most important idea in this whole lesson.

2. The Golden Rule: "of" vs. "increased/decreased by"

The single most common SAT percent trap
"X% of a number" is a straight multiplication.
"Increased/decreased BY X%" means you must add that amount back onto (or take it off of) the original number.
The trap: when a question says "the result of increasing 5 by 500% is x," almost every student computes \(500\%\times 5 = 25\) and moves on. That's wrong. 500% is only the amount it grew by — not the final answer.
Worked example — "The result of increasing the quantity 5 by 500% is x. What is x?"

You have to take the original number and add on the amount it increased by:

\(x = 5 + 500\%\text{ of }5 = 5+25=30\)

Here's the shortcut that makes this fast every time: 5 is just 100% of itself. So "5 plus 500% of 5" is the same as "100% of 5 plus 500% of 5," which is just 600% of 5 — and \(600\%\times5=30\). Same answer, one multiplication instead of two steps.

Desmos solution: no equation needed — just type 5 + 500% of 5 directly into the calculator and it evaluates to 30 on the spot.

x = 30 (not 25)

So the real rule is: increasing by x% = multiplying by (100+x)%. Increasing by 500% means multiplying by 600%. Increasing by 7% means multiplying by 107%. Always add the x% onto the 100% that's already "built in" to the original number.

3. Decreasing: 100% − x%

The mirror image of section 2
Decreasing by x% = multiplying by (100−x)%
Worked example — "The result of decreasing 5 by 20% is x. What is x?"

You can't just compute 20% of 5. You have to take the original number and subtract the amount it decreased by:

\(x = 5 - 20\%\text{ of }5 = 5-1=4\)

Using the shortcut: 5 is 100% of itself, so "5 minus 20% of 5" is "100% − 20% = 80% of 5," and \(80\%\times5=4\). Same answer.

Desmos solution: type 5 - 20% of 5 (or just 80% of 5) straight into the calculator — no equation-solving needed, it computes 4 immediately.

x = 4, and 80% of 5 gets you there in one step
Whenever you see a percentage in a question, the very first thing to check is: is this increasing by / decreasing by, or is it a straight "X% of Y"? That one check decides how you set up the whole equation, and the SAT tests this distinction constantly.

4. Reverse Percentage — finding the original number

When the question gives you the result and asks for the starting number
original × (1 ± percent) = final result
Worked example — "A customer paid $53 for a jacket after a 6% sales tax was added. What was the price before tax?"

The instinct is to take 6% off of $53 — that's wrong, and it doesn't even match an answer choice if you try it (you'd get $49.82). The 6% tax was never charged on $53; it was charged on the price before tax, which is what we don't know yet. Call that \(x\).

\(x + 6\%\text{ of }x = 53 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1.06x = 53\)

On the SAT's Desmos, you'd type this almost exactly as written — x + 6% of x ~ 53 — and let the squiggly-equals regression solve for x.

x = $50

The lesson here: whatever the percent is "of," that's the unknown you solve for — never the number you were given at the end. The 6% was on the original price, not on $53, so $53 can only ever appear on the other side of the equation.

5. Chained Percentages — discount, then tax

Two percentages, back to back, each one on a different number
Worked example — "Alma bought a laptop at a 20% discount. She paid P dollars including 8% sales tax on the discounted price. What is the original price in terms of P?"

Let the original price be \(x\). Two things happen to it, in order:

1) A 20% discount — that's a decrease, so the discounted price is \(0.8x\) (using the section 3 shortcut: 100%−20%=80%).

2) An 8% sales tax on that discounted price — that's an increase, so the final price is \(0.8x \times 1.08\).

\(0.8x \times 1.08 = P \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \dfrac{P}{0.8\times1.08}\)

Desmos solution: since the answer is an expression in P rather than a number, regression won't directly apply — but you can still type x = P/(0.8*1.08) into Desmos, give P any test value (say \(P=100\)), and Desmos instantly computes the matching x, letting you check your algebra against the answer choices numerically.

Original price = P ÷ (0.8 × 1.08) — answer choice D
The two percentages here are each "on" a different number — the 20% discount is on the original price, the 8% tax is on the discounted price. Keeping track of which number each percent actually multiplies is the whole skill. You can never slide a percent onto a number it wasn't described as acting on.

6. Three-Number Percent Chains (the "bracket" pattern)

The hardest, most common pattern on the real SAT — shows up almost every test

Watch for this exact shape of wording: "A is x% of (B + C), and B is y% of C. What percent of B is A?" — three quantities, three percent relationships, and a "what percent" question at the end. When you see that shape, set up three separate equations and solve them all at once using Desmos's bracket-regression trick: put every left-hand side in one bracket, every right-hand side in the matching bracket, separated by commas.

Worked example — "The positive number a is 2,241% of the sum of the positive numbers b and c, and b is 83% of c. What percent of b is a?"

Three facts, three equations. Call the unknown percent \(w\):

\(a = 2241\%(b+c) \qquad b = 83\%\,c \qquad w\%\text{ of }b = a\)

Stack the left sides in one bracket and the right sides in the matching bracket, then let Desmos regress for every unknown letter at once:

[a, b, w% of b]  ~  [2241% of (b+c), 83% of c, a]

Desmos returns a value for every unknown in one shot: \(a\approx49.41,\ b=1,\ c\approx1.205,\ w=4941\).

w = 4,941% — answer choice D

This is also the pattern behind questions about rates or speeds phrased as percents of each other (object A's speed is some % of object B's, etc.) — same three-equation bracket setup, just different units.

7. The "Plug In 100" Trick

For questions that give you only percentages — no real numbers at all

Sometimes a question describes two quantities purely in terms of percent changes, and never gives you a single real number to start from. When that happens, invent one. Pick 100 for the starting value — it's the easiest number to take percentages of — and carry it through the whole problem. Since every quantity in the question is defined only by percentages relative to each other, the actual number you pick never affects the final percent answer.

Worked example — "A predator and a prey population start the week the same size. By the end of the week, the prey increased by 4,000% and the predator increased by 900%. The prey population was then p% greater than the predator population. Find p."

No real starting population is given — so invent one. Let both populations start at 100:

Prey at week's end: \(100+4000\%\text{ of }100 = 100+4000=4100\)

Predator at week's end: \(100+900\%\text{ of }100=100+900=1000\)

Now the question's last sentence is just another "increased by" relationship between the two results you just found:

\(1000 + p\%\text{ of }1000 = 4100\)

Desmos solution: type 1000 + p% of 1000 ~ 4100 — the squiggly-equals regression solves for p directly, giving 310.

p = 310
Catch both "increased by" phrases and the "p% greater than" at the very end — that last one is an increase relationship too, and it's easy to read right past it after solving the first two parts.

8. Percent of a Solution (Mixture Problems)

A rarer pattern, but worth knowing the formula cold
(concentration₁)(amount₁) + (concentration₂)(amount₂) = (final concentration)(total amount)
Worked example — "How many liters of a 25% saline solution must be added to 3 liters of a 10% saline solution to obtain a 15% saline solution?"

Let \(x\) be the unknown amount (liters) of the 25% solution. The total amount at the end is whatever you pour in plus the 3 liters already there, i.e. \(x+3\):

\(0.25x + 0.10(3) = 0.15(x+3)\)

Type it into Desmos almost exactly like that, with the equals sign as a squiggly regression, and solve for \(x\).

x = 1.5 liters

One-Variable Data on the SAT

Problem-Solving & Data Analysis

A one-variable data question gives you a single list of numbers — test scores, plant heights, number of books read — shown as a plain list, a dot plot, a histogram, a box plot, or a frequency table, and asks you something about its center (mean, median, mode), its spread (range, interquartile range, standard deviation), or how that center/spread changes when the data changes.

The hard part is almost never the arithmetic. It's reading the right number off the right kind of picture, and knowing which measures a picture can and can't actually tell you. This lesson goes through every picture type the SAT uses, in the order they show up, using real SAT questions (already in this practice set) worked all the way through.

1. Measures of Center: Mean, Median, Mode

Three different ways to describe "the middle," and why they don't always agree
Mean = (sum of all values) ÷ (how many values). Median = the middle value once the data is sorted (average the two middle values if there's an even count). Mode = the value that shows up most often.
Worked example — "What is the median of the data shown?"

Data: \(71, 72, 73, 76, 77, 79, 83, 87, 93\) — already sorted, 9 values.

With an odd count of \(n\) sorted values, the median is just the \(\frac{n+1}{2}\)-th value. Here \(n=9\), so it's the 5th value — count in from either end: 71, 72, 73, 76, 77.

median = 77

The median only cares about position, not size — so one huge or tiny value barely moves it. The mean cares about every value's actual size, so it's much more sensitive to an extreme number. Watch what happens when a big new value joins the group:

Worked example — comparing the mean of data set X and data set Y

Data set X: \(5, 9, 9, 13\). Data set Y is the same four numbers plus one more: \(5, 9, 9, 13, 27\).

Mean of X: \((5+9+9+13)\div 4 = 36 \div 4 = 9\).

Mean of Y: \((36+27)\div 5 = 63 \div 5 = 12.6\).

Desmos solution: type (5+9+9+13)/4 and (5+9+9+13+27)/5 as two separate lines and compare the two results directly — no need to simplify by hand.

The mean of X is LESS than the mean of Y (choice B)

One new value (27) dragged the mean up by more than 3 points. That sensitivity to extreme values is exactly what the next section is about.

2. Measures of Spread: Range and IQR

How far the data is stretched out, in two different ways
Range = maximum − minimum (the whole spread). Interquartile range (IQR) = Q3 − Q1 (just the middle 50% of the data).
Worked example — "What is the range of the 7 scores shown?"

Data (sorted): \(23, 27, 27, 32, 35, 36, 52\).

Range = maximum − minimum \(= 52 - 23 = 29\).

range = 29

Range uses only the two most extreme values, so a single freakishly high or low number can blow it up. IQR ignores the top and bottom quarters of the data entirely, so it's much more resistant to outliers — which is exactly why box plots (section 6) are built around the IQR instead of the range.

3. How an Outlier Pulls the Mean

The "total trick": turn a mean into a sum, then the arithmetic becomes easy

Whenever a question gives you a mean and a count, immediately multiply them to get the total (sum) — it's much easier to add, remove, or compare totals than to juggle means directly.

Worked example — "What was the highest score?"

The mean score of 8 players was 14.5, so the total of all 8 scores is \(14.5 \times 8 = 116\).

After removing the highest score, the mean of the remaining 7 players is 12, so their total is \(12 \times 7 = 84\).

The highest score is whatever was removed: \(116 - 84 = 32\).

Desmos solution: type 14.5*8 - 12*7 as one line — it computes both totals and subtracts them in a single step, giving 32.

highest score = 32 (choice C)

Here's a trickier version where you have to reason about an outlier's effect on several measures at once:

Worked example — which measure does NOT stay the same?

A data set of 27 different numbers has mean 33 and median 33. A new data set adds 7 to every number above the median and subtracts 7 from every number below the median.

Median: unchanged — the middle number was exactly 33 itself, and it isn't shifted by either rule, so the middle of the sorted list is still 33.

Sum and mean: unchanged — with 27 values and the median itself untouched, 13 values go up by 7 and 13 values go down by 7. Those changes cancel exactly, so the total sum (and therefore the mean) doesn't move.

Standard deviation: this is the one that changes — every value above the median moved even farther above it, and every value below moved even farther below it, so the data is now more spread out around the center, even though the center itself didn't move.

Standard deviation is the one that changes (choice D)

4. Dot Plots

Each dot is one data value; stacked dots mean that value showed up more than once

A dot plot puts every individual value on a number line and stacks a dot for each time it occurs — so unlike a histogram, you can always see the exact values, not just which range they fall in.

Worked example — shifting every value by the same amount

The dot plot shows the 15 values in data set A. Data set B is created by adding 56 to every value in A.

Adding the same constant to every value slides the whole distribution to the right — the middle value moves too, so the median of B is greater than the median of A.

But sliding a distribution doesn't stretch or squeeze it: if the two farthest-apart values in A were 4 units apart, they're still exactly 4 units apart after both get +56. So the range of B equals the range of A.

Median of B is greater; range of B is equal to range of A (choice C)
Worked example — comparing standard deviations from two dot plots

Two dot plots show the number of glue sticks brought in by students in class A and class B. Class B's dots are just class A's dots, shifted 13 units to the right — same exact pattern of stacking, just relabeled.

Same idea as the median/range example above: shifting a distribution changes where it's centered but never how spread out it is. Identical shape means identical standard deviation.

The standard deviations are EQUAL (choice B)

5. Histograms

Bars over value-ranges, not individual dots — you only know which "bucket" a value fell into

A histogram groups values into equal-width intervals ("bins") and draws a bar whose height is how many values landed in that bin. The trade-off versus a dot plot: you can instantly see how many values are in, say, the 40s — but you lose the exact values. That hidden information is exactly what the hardest histogram questions exploit.

Worked example — the smallest possible difference between two means

Two histograms each summarize 23 integers. Data set A's bins are [20,30), [30,40), [40,50), [50,60) with frequencies 3, 4, 7, 9. Data set B's bins are [10,20), [20,30), [30,40), [40,50) — the exact same frequencies 3, 4, 7, 9, just every bin shifted 10 lower.

Since a histogram only tells you the bin, not the exact value, you get to choose where inside each bin every value actually sits. To make the two means as close together as possible, push every A-value to the bottom of its bin (e.g. right at 20, 30, 40, 50) and every matching B-value to the top of its bin (e.g. just under 20, 30, 40, 50 — so 19, 29, 39, 49, since these are integers).

Every matched pair then differs by exactly 1 (e.g. \(20-19=1\)), so the smallest possible difference between the two means is 1 — it can't be pushed to 0, because A's values can never actually dip into B's bin range or vice versa.

smallest possible difference = 1 (choice B)

6. Box Plots

The five-number summary — min, Q1, median, Q3, max — drawn as a box with whiskers

A box plot's whiskers run from the minimum to the maximum; the box itself spans Q1 to Q3 (so the box width IS the IQR); the line inside the box is the median. That's all five numbers you can read directly — and that's all you can read. A box plot never tells you the mean, because it doesn't show individual values, only those five landmarks.

Worked example — which statement MUST be true?

Two box plots summarize the masses of two groups of gazelles. Group 1's median line sits higher on the number line than group 2's median line.

That comparison is directly readable — group 1's median really is greater than group 2's median, no assumptions needed.

A statement comparing the two means, on the other hand, can look tempting from the picture but is never guaranteed — box plots don't show enough information to pin down the mean unless you're told the data is perfectly symmetric.

The median of group 1 is greater than the median of group 2 (choice C)
Worked example — reading the range straight off two box plots

Class A's whiskers run from 0 to 5, so its range is \(5-0=5\). Class B's whiskers run from 1 to 10, so its range is \(10-1=9\).

Positive difference between the ranges: \(9-5=4\).

Desmos solution: type (10-1) - (5-0) as one line and it computes both ranges and their difference at once, giving 4.

difference = 4

7. Frequency Tables and Bar Graphs

A compact way to show how many times each value occurs

A frequency table lists every value in one column and how many times it occurred in the next; a bar graph draws the same idea as bars instead of numbers. Either way, the total count is just the sum of all the frequencies/bar heights.

Worked example — reading a bar graph directly

The bar graph shows 419 cans collected by 10 groups. How many cans did group 6 collect? You don't need to calculate anything — just read the height of the bar labeled "6" straight off the vertical axis.

group 6 collected 40 cans

This table summarizes 57 values (check: \(3+3+8+8+9+11+9+0+6=57\), so every value is accounted for). What's the maximum value in the data set?

The maximum data value is the largest value that actually occurs — i.e. has a nonzero frequency. The value 13 is listed but its frequency is 0, meaning nobody actually has that value; 14 has frequency 6, so 14 really does occur.

mean − median = 0 (choice A)

8. Standard Deviation, Without the Formula

The SAT never makes you calculate it — it makes you compare it

Standard deviation measures how far, typically, the values sit from the mean. A small standard deviation means the values are tightly clustered close to the mean; a large one means they're spread out, with some values sitting far away. You will never need the standard deviation formula on the SAT — every real question just asks you to compare which of two or three data sets is more or less spread out, usually from a list, dot plot, or histogram.

Worked example — ranking three data sets by spread

Data Set 1: \(\{1,1,1,3,3,3,3,5,6,8\}\) — values range all the way from 1 to 8, several sitting far from the middle.

Data Set 2: \(\{2,2,3,3,3,4,4,4,5,5\}\) — tighter, values only range from 2 to 5.

Data Set 3: \(\{3,3,3,3,3,4,4,4,4,4\}\) — every single value is only 3 or 4; nothing could be more tightly clustered.

Largest standard deviation: Data Set 1. Smallest standard deviation: Data Set 3.

9. Combining Two Data Sets — the Weighted Mean

You can't just average the two means — the bigger group pulls harder
combined mean = (sum of group 1 + sum of group 2) ÷ (count 1 + count 2), where sum = mean × count
Worked example — merging two data sets

Data set A: 75 buildings, mean height 32 m → total height \(= 75\times32=2400\) m.

Data set B: 50 buildings, mean height 62 m → total height \(=50\times62=3100\) m.

Data set C is all 125 buildings combined: total \(=2400+3100=5500\) m, so the mean is \(5500\div125=44\) m.

Desmos solution: type (75*32 + 50*62)/(75+50) as one line — it computes both totals, adds them, and divides by the combined count in a single step, giving 44.

mean of C = 44 meters
Don't just average the two means! \((32+62)\div2=47\) is wrong — it secretly pretends both groups have the same number of buildings. Group A has more buildings (75 vs. 50), so it pulls the true combined mean (44) down closer to its own mean (32) than to group B's.

Ratios, Rates, and Units on the SAT

Problem-Solving & Data Analysis

This skill covers four closely related ideas: ratios (comparing two quantities), rates (a ratio where one quantity is "per" some unit, like speed or density), unit conversion (rewriting the same quantity in different units), and proportional relationships (two quantities that scale together). On the SAT they all reduce to the same move: set up a fraction, multiply or divide by the right conversion factor, and keep track of what cancels.

This lesson walks through every version of that move using real SAT questions (already in this practice set) worked all the way through, with a Desmos shortcut for every example that has one.

1. Ratios — Keeping a Ratio Fixed

If a ratio has to stay the same, both parts scale by the same factor
If \(\dfrac{a}{b}\) must stay constant and \(b\) changes to \(b'\), then \(a\) must change to \(a\times\dfrac{b'}{b}\).
Worked example — "how must the length change to maintain this ratio?"

The ratio of length to width is 35 to 10. If the width increases by 7 units (from 10 to 17), the length must scale by the same factor, \(\frac{17}{10}\), to keep the ratio at 35:10.

Original length (using width = 10 as the base): \(35\). New length: \(35\times\frac{17}{10}=59.5\).

Change in length: \(59.5-35=24.5\), and it's an increase, since the width increased.

Desmos solution: type 35*(17/10) - 35 as one line — it computes the new length and subtracts the old one in a single step, giving 24.5.

The length must increase by 24.5 units (choice B)
Don't just copy the amount the width changed by (7 units) onto the length — that only works if the ratio is 1 to 1. Here the length moves by \(7\times\frac{35}{10}=24.5\) units, not 7.

2. Unit Rates

A rate is just a ratio with "per" built in — distance per time, posters per minute, etc.
rate × amount of the "per" unit = total. So total ÷ rate = amount of the "per" unit, and total ÷ amount = rate.
Worked example — constant speed

An object travels at a constant 12 centimeters per second. How long does it take to travel 108 centimeters?

Time = distance ÷ rate \(=108\div12=9\) seconds.

Desmos solution: type 108/12 directly.

9 seconds (choice A)
Worked example — converting a rate's time unit

A printer makes 42 posters per minute. What's that rate in posters per hour? Since there are 60 minutes in an hour, multiply the per-minute rate by 60.

\(42\times60=2520\) posters per hour.

Desmos solution: type 42*60 directly.

2,520 posters per hour

3. Unit Conversion

Rewriting the same quantity in different-sized units
Converting to a smaller unit → multiply by the conversion factor. Converting to a bigger unit → divide by it.
Worked example — meters to centimeters

A centimeter is smaller than a meter (1 meter = 100 centimeters), so converting 51 meters to centimeters means multiplying by 100: \(51\times100=5{,}100\) cm.

Desmos solution: type 51*100 directly.

5,100 centimeters (choice C)

Going the other way — centimeters to meters, a bigger unit — means dividing instead: \(2{,}300\div100=23\) meters.

4. Chained Conversions, and Squared/Cubed Units

When one conversion factor isn't enough — or the units are squared

Some questions give you the conversion in two steps (furlongs → yards → feet). Just chain the multiplications in one line instead of finding a single combined factor yourself.

Worked example — furlongs to feet

1 furlong = 220 yards, and 1 yard = 3 feet. So 1 furlong = \(220\times3=660\) feet.

354 furlongs \(=354\times220\times3\) feet.

Desmos solution: type 354*220*3 as one line — no need to compute the 660-feet-per-furlong factor separately first.

233,640 feet (choice D)
When the unit itself is squared (square yards, square miles) or cubed, the conversion factor gets squared or cubed too — it does NOT apply just once.
Worked example — square yards to square miles

1 mile = 1,760 yards, so 1 square mile \(=1{,}760^2\) square yards — not just 1,760. To convert square yards to square miles, divide by \(1{,}760^2\), not 1,760.

\(11{,}863{,}808 \div 1{,}760^2 \approx 3.83\) square miles.

Desmos solution: type 11863808/1760^2 — the exponent handles the "squared unit" automatically.

3.83 square miles (choice B)

5. Density = Mass ÷ Volume

A rate you're expected to recognize by name, not just by formula
density = mass ÷ volume, so mass = density × volume, and volume = mass ÷ density. For a cube with edge length \(s\): volume \(=s^3\).
Worked example — finding mass from density

A cube of oak has density 807 kg/m³ and edge length 0.90 m. Its volume is \(0.90^3=0.729\) m³.

Mass = density × volume \(=807\times0.729\approx588\) kg.

Desmos solution: type 807*0.9^3 as one line.

588 kg (choice A)
Worked example — working backward to find edge length

A cube of wood has density 353 kg/m³ and mass 345 kg. First find its volume: volume = mass ÷ density \(=345\div353\) m³. Then undo the cube to get the edge length: take the cube root.

Desmos solution: type (345/353)^(1/3) — raising to the \(\frac{1}{3}\) power takes the cube root in one step.

edge ≈ 0.99 m (choice B)

6. Proportional Relationships as Expressions

When the question wants a formula, not a number — plug in an easy test value

Some questions ask for an expression that represents a rate or ratio, with variables instead of numbers in the answer choices. The fastest way through these: invent a simple number for the variable, work out what the answer should be by hand, then check which choice gives that same number when you substitute.

Worked example — coaches to athletes

The ratio of coaches to athletes is 1 to 26. With \(x\) coaches, how many athletes (as an expression in \(x\))?

Test it with an easy number: if \(x=1\) coach, there should be 26 athletes (since the ratio is 1 to 26). Only 26x gives 26 when \(x=1\) — the other choices give \(\frac{1}{26}\), 27, and 26 respectively, and only one of those matches by coincidence, so always double check with the real ratio in mind.

Desmos solution: define \(x=1\), then type each answer choice as its own line (e.g. 26x, x/26, x+26) and see which one evaluates to 26.

\(26x\) (choice B)

7. Two Linked Ratios — Solve for the Missing One

When only the ratio between letters matters, you can pick convenient values for them

If you're told \(\frac{x}{y}=4\) and nothing else pins down \(x\) and \(y\) individually, you're free to pick any pair of numbers that satisfies that ratio — like \(x=4,\ y=1\) — and the rest of the problem will still work out, because only the ratio ever mattered in the first place.

Worked example — "what is the value of n?"

\(\dfrac{x}{y}=4\) and \(\dfrac{24x}{ny}=4\). Since only \(\frac{x}{y}\) matters, let \(x=4,\ y=1\) (any pair with ratio 4 works).

Substitute: \(\dfrac{24(4)}{n(1)}=4\).

Desmos solution: type 24*4/n ~ 4 — the squiggly-equals regression solves for n directly, giving 24.

n = 24
Worked example — the same trick with decimals

\(\dfrac{4a}{b}=6.7\) and \(\dfrac{a}{bn}=26.8\). From the first equation, \(\dfrac{a}{b}=\dfrac{6.7}{4}=1.675\), so let \(a=1.675,\ b=1\).

Desmos solution: type 1.675/n ~ 26.8 — this solves for n directly, giving 0.0625 (equivalently \(\frac{1}{16}\)).

n = 0.0625 (or \(\frac{1}{16}\))

8. Rates of Change, Combined with Conversion

The hardest version: a rate that also needs a unit conversion

These stack two skills from this lesson — first set up the rate relationship, then convert the units, usually in one combined Desmos line.

Worked example — splitting a total flux between two squares

A surface is made of a small square and a large square, where the large square's side is 3 times the small square's side. Electric flux = field strength × area, and the total flux through both squares is 4,640 (with field strength 29). Since area scales with side-length squared, the large square has \(3^2=9\) times the area of the small one, so the two areas split the total flux in a 1-to-9 ratio.

Total "area units": \(1+9=10\). The large square gets \(\frac{9}{10}\) of the total flux.

Desmos solution: type 4640*(9/10) — giving 4,176 directly, no need to solve for the side lengths at all.

4,176 volts·meters
Worked example — converting a rate of change's units

An area is growing at 250 square feet per hour. Convert to square meters per minute: divide by \(3.28^2\) to go from square feet to square meters, then divide by 60 to go from per-hour to per-minute.

Desmos solution: type 250/(3.28^2*60) as one line, giving about 0.39.

≈ 0.39 square meters per minute (choice A)
Worked example — converting an acceleration's units

A vehicle's speed increases at 7.3 meters per second². Convert to miles per minute²: divide by 1,609 to go from meters to miles, then multiply by \(60^2=3{,}600\) to go from "per second squared" to "per minute squared" (since 1 minute = 60 seconds, converting a squared time unit means squaring the 60).

Desmos solution: type 7.3/1609*3600 as one line, giving about 16.3.

≈ 16.3 miles per minute² (choice B)
